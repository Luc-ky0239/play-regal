How the peg pyramid works

The board is a triangle of staggered pegs, with each new row a peg wider than the one above it. A ball released at the top has an even chance of moving left or right at every peg, then repeats that split all the way down. After the final row, it lands in one of the bottom pockets — always one more pocket than the number of rows.

Because each deflection is a fifty-fifty step, most balls cluster around the middle and only a few reach the edges. Statistically, the setup behaves like a Galton board, with a binomial distribution behind it. The payout table is the same curve turned upside down: common middle pockets pay less, while uncommon outer pockets pay more.

Multipliers, odds, and the house margin

Each pocket value is set to reflect its probability, then trimmed slightly for the house margin. When the payout for every pocket is weighed against its chance of landing, the total comes out just under one, which is how the operator keeps a margin. Most versions aimed at UK players publish a return to player somewhere around 97% to 99%, depending on the studio and the chosen settings.

A simple truth helps here: changing rows or risk does not improve the long-run return to the player. Those settings only change the size and shape of the swings, not the average result over time.

Six words worth knowing

Pocket
The slot at the bottom of the board that holds the multiplier for a completed drop.
Rows
The number of peg lines the ball passes through; more rows mean more pockets and tighter odds.
Risk level
A preset that stretches or compresses the payout table without changing the overall return much.
House margin
The small gap between fair odds and paid odds that pays the operator.
Provably fair
A system of published hashes that lets players check whether a result was set in advance.
Variance
The amount of movement around the average result — the part the risk control really changes.

Straight answers to five questions

Is every drop genuinely random?

On licensed sites, yes. The path is driven by an audited random number generator, and provably fair systems let you verify after the round that the result was fixed before the ball moved.

Do more rows make the game pay better?

No. Extra rows add pockets and spread the odds curve, so edge payouts rise while their chance of landing falls. The overall return stays broadly the same.

Which risk level is the smart choice?

None is better mathematically. Low risk smooths the session, while high risk puts more value into rare edge hits. Choose the setting that matches your budget and your tolerance for swings.
